When I was seventeen, everyone in my family already knew what I would become. My grandmother called me “the doctor” before I had even finished secondary school. I liked the sound of it. It felt safe, like a road that had already been paved.

So I followed it. I studied biology. I memorized the bones of the hand and the names of every muscle in the leg. I was good at exams. But every evening, when I closed my textbooks, I picked up something else: a pencil.

I drew the people on the bus. The woman selling mangoes at the corner. My little brother, asleep with his mouth open. Drawing was the only time the noise in my head went quiet.

In my second year, a friend asked me to design a poster for a community health day. I stayed up all night. When I saw people stop in the street to read it, something shifted. I had helped someone understand an idea, not with a prescription, but with a picture.

Telling my family was the hardest conversation of my life. My mother was silent for a long time. Then she asked one question: “Are you running away from something, or towards something?”

I said, “Towards.” And I meant it.

Today I work as an illustrator for health education campaigns. I still use what I learned about the human body. I still think about my grandmother’s hopes. The road I am on was not paved for me. I have had to build it, one step at a time.

If you are standing at the start of a road someone else chose for you, I won’t tell you to leave it. I’ll only ask you the question my mother asked me. Are you running away, or running towards?

Thank you for listening.
